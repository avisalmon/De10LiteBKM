Accelerometer 3D cube

Tilt the board and a shaded cube on the LCD tilts with it. The DE10-Lite's own ADXL345 accelerometer is read over SPI; a sequential math engine turns the direction of gravity into pitch, roll and a rotation matrix once per frame, inside the vertical blanking; a four-stage pipeline then colours every pixel as the screen scans, with no frame buffer. Next to the cube: the live acceleration as numbers and bars, the peak, and a 3D arrow. Every picture the design makes was first made by a bit-exact Python model; the simulation and the board captures match it pixel for pixel.

Three clocks come out of one PLL. The 25 MHz pixel clock runs everything the cube does: the filter and the controls (cube_core), the per-frame math engine (cube_math), the pixel pipeline (cube_draw, cube_text), the USB link and the report line. The 50 MHz clock runs only the SPI reader of the ADXL345 (adxl345): a sample crosses to 25 MHz through a held value and a toggle. The 100 MHz clock belongs to the library's LCD writer inside Screens_dispaly. The biggest user is the math engine, 8,245 logic elements and 4,654 registers: it is one step-by-step machine that keeps the whole frame's results (12 edge functions of 48 bits, 8 line segments with their boxes, 7 BCD numbers) in registers and shares one 18×18 multiplier, one divider, one square root, one CORDIC and one BCD converter between all its steps. The pixel pipeline is next, 3,626 logic elements and 80 of the 82 multiplier elements: it evaluates 20 line equations for every pixel, each a pair of 16×18-bit products. Nearly all the memory, 921,600 bits in 114 M9K blocks, is the screen capture buffer of debug_link (every 2nd pixel and line, 320 × 240 × 12 bits): it exists only so a PC can read the picture back over USB for the tests; the design itself needs 3 blocks (the LCD command list and the JTAG UART).

What you will see

Plug the board in with the LCD shield on and load the design. The left 384 pixels hold the cube on a sky gradient, seen from 30° above. Lay the board flat: the cube sits level, its blue +Z face on top. Lift the far edge: the cube pitches towards you; lift a side: it rolls. The cube follows the filtered reading (one eighth of the way to each new sample), so it moves smoothly and ignores a tap. The right panel shows the raw reading: X, Y and Z in g with a bar each (1 g = 56 pixels), the magnitude, the highest magnitude since the last restart (PEAK), and pitch and roll in degrees. Below the numbers a 3D arrow shows the same raw vector in a small axis cross, 1 g = 64 pixels, with its dashed projection to the floor; shake the board and the arrow and bars jump while the cube stays calm. The last row says LIVE, LIVE CAL after you calibrate (KEY1 with the board flat), or TEST n for one of the eight test presets you can call up over USB. The 7-segment digits show the mode letter and |a| in g; the LEDs are a bubble level along x. Letters typed over the USB cable (de10.py send) do everything the keys do, and r prints one report line with every number.

Block diagram

Two clock crossings, both one way. The ADXL345 reader lives on the 50 MHz clock; when it has a new sample it holds x, y, z in registers and flips one bit. Three flip-flops bring that bit to 25 MHz, and a change of the bit is the one-clock acc_valid pulse; by then the held values have been stable for at least two 50 MHz clocks, so they are read plainly. The keys are two-flop synchronised, as usual. The LCD writer runs at 100 MHz inside the library's Screens_dispaly, which takes care of its own crossing. The blocks are split by when they work: cube_core at every sample (100 Hz), cube_math once per frame (60 Hz) in the 28,000 idle clocks between the start of vertical sync and the first visible pixel, cube_draw at every pixel (25 MHz). Only the per-pixel part has to be parallel; everything else is one sequential machine sharing one multiplier, which is what keeps the design at a quarter of the chip.

How it flows

  1. A sample arrives. Every 10 ms the ADXL345 has new x, y, z (1 g = 256 counts). adxl345 reads them over 4-wire SPI at 50 MHz and flips its toggle; cube_core sees acc_valid at 25 MHz. The filter moves one eighth of the way to the new sample (fx += (x − fx) / 8 in 1/256 counts) and rounds to Q4 counts (1 g = 4096): that is g. The raw sample shifted left by 4 is a. Both lose the calibration offsets (zero until KEY1 / c), and the HEADING parameter turns x, y into the screen's frame.
  2. The frame tick. At the falling edge of VGA_VS the screen has finished its last visible line and the 10-line front porch; the sync and the back porch, 35 blank lines (28,000 clocks), follow before the first visible pixel. cube_math latches g, a, the view yaw and the peak-restart request, and runs its phases: squares and square roots give |a| and |g|; if |g| ≥ 0.25 g the CORDIC (vectoring) gives pitch and roll, otherwise the last orientation is held; the CORDIC (rotation) gives cos and sin of the view yaw; the rotation matrix is multiplied out; the 8 corners are projected with the divider (perspective, camera 600 px away); the faces whose normal points at the camera get a shade from the light and 4 edge functions E(x, y) = ((x·dy − y·dx) << 4) + c; x, y, z, |a|, the peak, pitch and roll become BCD; the arrow, its head, its floor shadow and the three axes become 8 line segments with bounding boxes. 876 to 948 clocks later done rises, with 35,000 clocks to spare.
  3. Every pixel. As the visible lines scan, cube_draw works 4 pixels ahead of the one the screen asks for: stage 0 picks the coordinates (and knows the counter's quirks in the blanking), stage 1 multiplies them into the 12 edge functions and 8 segment functions, stage 2 decides inside / on-the-edge / outside, stage 3 picks the colour by priority: a face (darkened on its edges) or the sky gradient left of x = 384, the separator, then on the right the widget (segments, then the X Y Z labels) below y = 224 or the text panel above it (a glyph from cube_text and the 8×8 font at 2×, the bars, the ticks). The registered colour leaves as rgb; Screens_dispaly writes it to the LCD at 100 MHz.
  4. Letters and lines. A letter typed on the PC comes through jtag_uart and debug_link: ? and F are answered there (status, screen capture); the others reach cube_core: a digit freezes the engine's inputs on a test preset, l goes back to the sensor, c / u take or forget the offsets, s starts the view turning 0.5° a frame, k restarts the peak, r asks for the report. Between two frames line_printer sends the 114 characters of the report through tx_mux and the same USB cable. KEY0 restarts the peak, KEY1 calibrates. g along x lights one of the ten LEDs (a bubble level); |a| and the mode letter are on the 7-segment digits.

How it was tested

Without the board

The reference is assets/model.py: the whole design in Python with the same integers, bit for bit. Its selftest checks the integer math against floating point (angles within 0.1°, the CORDIC gain, the perspective), the filter from both sides, the saturation, the two-vector split and the report length. Two testbenches then hold the RTL to the model. sim/tb_cube_math.sv runs the engine alone for 18 frames: the 8 presets in sequence, then 8 more vectors with other view yaws (both sides of the CORDIC's ±90° pre-rotation, ±180°, a peak kept from an earlier frame) and two live cases; it prints every output of every frame (569 lines) and compares them with sim/math_expected.txt, written by model.py dump; PASS, about one second. sim/tb_accel_cube.sv runs the whole cube_core with the library's VGA counter and captures 13 frames to sim/frame_*.ppm, each the way the board's capture does it (every 2nd pixel and line, 320 × 240 = 76,800 pixels): the 8 presets, then the live path (120 samples through the filter), a KEY1 calibration, the frame after one more sample, the r report (114 characters + CR LF, compared character by character), u, a frame while spinning, and the spin stopping; each frame's hash must equal the model's. PASS: 13 frames bit-exact with the model, calibration, report and spin checked, about 20 minutes in Icarus. A line-by-line dump beats a frame hash: review item 8 below was a disagreement that drew the same (empty) pixels and only the dump saw it.

On the board

The board was programmed and each preset called up over USB, the screen read back and compared with the model: de10.py send "3" 1, de10.py screen logs/board_3.png, model.py compare seq:3 logs/board_3.txt. All eight presets: 0 of 76,800 pixels differ, in both the first version and the rework. Then the live path with the board flat on the table: r gave Z+1.000 MAG 1.000 ... ID E5 LIVE; c gave Z+0.998 MAG 0.998 PITCH +0.1 ROLL -0.0 ... LIVE CAL and u went back; s made two captures a second apart differ with the cube visibly turned, s again stopped it; after k a 60-second watch of r showed PEAK steady at 1.009..1.012 g, so the raw path has no glitch samples. The ? status line (T=25B2 BTN=00 KEY=0 SW=3FF WHL=000) came from debug_link as in every project.

Only a person can check

Which way the cube leans when you lift a given edge (the HEADING parameter, 0 by default, chooses where the board's +x points on the screen), that nose-down reads as a negative pitch, which way the LED bubble slides, and the feel of KEY1 (the USB c path is verified on the board; the key itself only in simulation). One PEAK reading of 2.359 g appeared once between two command batches with the board on the desk; it never recurred in the 60-second watch and was most likely a knock on the desk. The webcam capture (de10.py cam) failed on the build PC, so there is no board photo yet.
