Accelerometer 3D cube: architecture spec

What each module is, what crosses between them, where every bit of state lives, and the budgets. Written for someone who will change the design or build the next one on it. The arithmetic is specified by assets/model.py: whatever it computes, the RTL computes bit for bit, and the tests hold them together.

Requirements

  • Read the DE10-Lite's ADXL345 at its own rate (100 Hz, 1 g = 256 counts, 4-wire SPI) and check its device ID (0xE5).
  • Show on the 640 × 480 LCD, at the display's 59.5 frames a second and with no frame buffer: a shaded, perspective 3D cube whose orientation is the direction of gravity; the acceleration as signed numbers in g with three decimals, as bars and as a 3D arrow; its magnitude and the highest magnitude since a restart; pitch and roll in tenths of a degree.
  • The cube must move smoothly and ignore a tap (a filtered vector); the numbers, bars and arrow must show what the board feels right now, shakes included (the raw vector). Both from the same sensor, both minus the same calibration offsets.
  • Hold the orientation when gravity is too weak to define it (|g| < 0.25 g, for example in free fall) and hold the roll when the board stands on its x edge; never jump to a random angle.
  • All per-frame math must finish between the start of vertical sync and the first visible pixel (35 blank lines, 28,000 clocks at 25 MHz); the pixel pipeline must produce one colour per clock with no visible seam at the left edge of a line.
  • Controls without a PC: the shield's game pad (the shield covers the board's KEY0 and KEY1): A calibrates, B restarts the peak, Select forgets the calibration, Start spins the view; KEY0 and KEY1 do the same as B and A for a bare board. Over USB: the same, plus eight test presets, back to live, and a one-line report of every number.
  • Testable without hands: a bit-exact model of every picture and number; frame hashes in simulation; the screen read back over USB (debug_link's F) and the report line (r) compared with the model on the board.
  • Board: DE10-Lite (MAX 10 10M50) with the LCD / game-pad shield. Tools: Quartus Prime Lite 17.1, Icarus Verilog or ModelSim for the testbenches, Python 3 with Pillow for the model. No licensed IP: the PLL is altpll, the LCD command list a ROM.

The frame tick is made in the pixel domain itself: tick = vs_d && !VGA_VS, the first clock of vertical sync. The library counter puts vertical sync after the 10-line front porch and ends every line with its blanking, so from tick to the first visible pixel of the next frame there are the sync and the back porch: 35 lines × 800 clocks = 28,000 clocks at 25 MHz (the whole blanking is 45 lines, 36,000 clocks). The engine uses at most 948 of them.

State machines and encodings

The engine, cube_math: phase[3:0], with a step counter k[4:0] and an index idx[3:0]

One multiplier (ma × mb, 18 × 18 → 40 bits, one clock), one divider, one square root, one CORDIC and one BCD converter serve every phase. The pattern inside a phase is load the operands at step k, use the product at step k + 1; a step that waits for a unit keeps k (k <= k). The 876 to 948 clocks a frame measured in simulation depend only on how many faces are visible (3 or 2).

The pixel pipeline, cube_draw

Four register stages, so the colour for (x, y) leaves exactly when the library asks for it. Stage 0 computes the coordinates of the pixel 4 clocks ahead from the library counter, which is the only subtle part: the counter holds column 639 through the 160-clock horizontal blanking and then jumps to column 0 of the next row, so a counter of clocks spent at 639 (hold_cnt) tells when the lookahead must already be at columns 0..3 of the next row; the row holds 479 through the vertical blanking and wraps to 0 together with the column.

The sensor protocol

adxl345 (lib, read-accelerometer) writes the configuration after power-up (DATA_FORMAT 0x08: full resolution, ±2 g; BW_RATE 0x0A: 100 Hz; POWER_CTL 0x08: measure), reads DEVID (register 0x00, must be 0xE5), then reads X, Y, Z (six registers) every 10 ms. The values are 13-bit two's complement at 3.9 mg a count, sign-extended to 16 bits: 1 g = 256. SPI mode 3 at 1 MHz (the clock idles high, data changes on the falling edge and is sampled on the rising).

Design decisions

  • No frame buffer. A 640 × 480 × 12-bit buffer is 3.7 Mbit, twice the chip's memory, and the SDRAM would have cost a controller and a clock domain. Instead the picture is a function of (x, y) and a few hundred bits of frame parameters: a cube is 3 convex polygons, a line is a strip, text is a font lookup. Everything is drawn from math, so there is also nothing to clear and no tearing: every frame is complete.
  • One sequential engine with one multiplier instead of parallel arithmetic: the math needs about 140 multiplies a frame and has 28,000 clocks to do them in. The price is the step tables in cube_math; the gain is 8,265 LEs instead of several times that, and a design that fits the Lite board with room for more.
  • A bit-exact Python model first. Every rounding, saturation and shift was decided in model.py, then copied into the RTL. The payoff is that a frame hash in simulation and a pixel compare on the board are exact tests, not "looks right". The cost is that a change must be made twice; model.py constants prints every number the RTL copies.
  • Two vectors from one sensor. The filtered g (an exponential average, 1/8 a sample: 63 % in 8 samples, 1 % after 35) drives the cube, the angles and the bubble, so they move smoothly and a tap does not spin the cube. The raw a drives the arrow, the bars and the numbers, so a shake is seen. The calibration offsets are subtracted from both.
  • The EMA keeps 8 fraction bits and rounds on the way out. In raw counts the 1/8 step would truncate to zero for small errors and the filter would stop 7 counts short; with the fraction bits it settles within 7/256 of a count, and the rounding (f + 8) >> 4 to Q4 hides even that.
  • Hold below 0.25 g. Dividing by a tiny |g| gives any angle; the model and the RTL keep the last sines and cosines instead, and the roll alone when the board stands on its x edge (r < 1/16 g). Preset 4 and 7 test both holds.
  • Q4 counts, Q14, BAM16, Q4 px. One fixed-point format per kind of quantity, each chosen so that the largest value fits 16 bits with the 18 × 18 multiplier: ±8 g, unit vectors, a full turn, and the 640-pixel screen in 1/16 steps. Angles in BAM wrap for free; the CORDIC carries 4 extra fraction bits so the atan table is exact enough.
  • Edge functions, not scan conversion. A convex face is the AND of four half-planes; evaluating x·dy − y·dx + c per pixel costs two multiplies per edge and no per-line setup. The constant c is computed once a frame at the pixel's centre, so the pipeline needs only the products with x and y.
  • Calibration in registers only. The offsets live in ox, oy, oz and are gone at power-up. Writing them to the flash would need the UFM and a second design decision about when to trust them; the board is flat in a second, and c is one letter. The offsets are taken in the sensor frame, before HEADING, so they are right for any heading.
  • Spin off by default, s to turn on. The first version spun always; a spinning cube hides whether it tilts right. The fixed view (yaw 30°, camera 30° above) shows three faces of a flat board: blue +Z on top.
  • No yaw from the sensor. Gravity defines pitch and roll only; a heading needs a magnetometer or a gyro the board does not have. The YAW row of the first version was dropped rather than show a made-up number.
  • HEADING as a parameter. Which way the board's +x points on the screen depends on how the shield sits; a rotation of 0, 90, 180 or 270° is applied to both vectors by a parameter, so a different mounting is a one-word change and a rebuild, and model.py takes the same number. Checked by hand with the LCD readable: HEADING 0 is right. The sensor's x runs left–right along the board and y near–far; lifting the right edge 22° gave X +0.38 g and PITCH −22°, lifting the near edge 33° gave Y −0.54 g and ROLL −33°, and the cube rose on the same side as the board both times.
  • The debug buffer stays in the build. 114 M9K are a lot, but the pixel-exact board test is worth more than the memory this project does not need. Anyone building on this design and needing the memory removes debug_link and the F test; SYNTH_RESOURCE_AWARE_INFERENCE_FOR_BLOCK_RAM OFF keeps Quartus from quietly turning the library's memories into flip-flops because of it.
