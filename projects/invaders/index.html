Space Invaders

The 1978 arcade game, played on the LCD shield with its game pad, in pure Verilog: no CPU, no program and no frame buffer.

Runs on the board Quartus Prime Lite 17.1 LCD / game-pad shield needed (the screen and the controls) 7,170 logic elements (14 %) Skill: build-arcade-game
Download invaders.qar (full Quartus project) Build and load it
OverviewArchitecture specUser guide

Once per frame, inside the vertical blanking, one game step moves everything by the arcade's rules, in at most 1,351 of the blank's 28,000 clocks; then every pixel is worked out from the game's registers as the screen scans it. The screen and the game ask the same logic what is lit at a pixel, so the shot hits exactly what you see. The rules come from the commented disassembly of the arcade's program and from MAME; the pictures are our own, at the arcade's sizes. Ten test presets reach known moments of a game, and the board's screen, read back over USB, matches the simulation pixel for pixel.

No bitstream in the repo (a .sof is 3 MB per project): restore the .qar or use the sources here, one build makes it in a few minutes. The user guide has every command.

Three clocks come out of one PLL (the second PLL and the ADC block belong to the library's game-pad reader, which makes its own 10 MHz for the MAX 10 ADC). The 25 MHz pixel clock runs the whole game: the step sequencer (inv_logic), the picture (inv_draw), the buzzer (inv_sound), the 7-segment digits and the USB link. The 50 MHz clock runs the game pad (gamepad with its 5 ms debounce; the eight buttons cross into 25 MHz through two flops each). The 100 MHz clock belongs to the library's LCD writer inside Screens_dispaly. The biggest user is inv_logic, 4,014 logic elements and 873 registers: the sequencer and the whole game state (55 alive bits, the 224-bit ground line, the rack's two references, the shot, three bombs, the saucer, the scores), and a second copy of inv_layers (461 logic elements), the probe through which the game asks the screen's own logic what is lit at a pixel. inv_draw is next, 1,197 logic elements and no registers at all: one combinational path from the scan counters to the colour, with at least 16.0 ns to spare in the 40 ns of a pixel (the slowest 25 MHz path runs through it into the screen-capture memory). No multipliers. Nearly all the memory, 921,600 bits in 114 M9K blocks, is the screen capture buffer of debug_link (every 2nd pixel and line, 320 × 240 × 12 bits): it exists only so a PC can read the picture back over USB for the tests. The game itself needs 6 blocks (33,536 bits): its text RAM and shield RAM (2), the LCD command list (2), the JTAG UART (1) and the ADC's sample store (1).

What you will see

Plug the board in with the shield on and load the design. It starts in the attract mode, as the arcade does: the score line, PLAY and SPACE INVADERS typed one letter at a time, the score advance table, a short silent demo game in which the cannon plays itself, INSERT COIN, and round again. Select on the game pad is a coin and Start starts a one-player game (KEY1 and KEY0 do the same on a bare board; the shield covers them). 55 invaders in five rows march sideways, one invader per frame, the ripple of the arcade, and step down 8 lines at each edge; the fewer are left, the faster they march. The joystick moves the cannon and A or B fires, one shot at a time. Three kinds of bombs fall; the four shields wear away pixel by pixel where shots and bombs burst; the saucer crosses the red band at the top, worth 50 to 300 points by the arcade's shot-count table. The colours are the arcade's cellophane overlay, bands across a one-bit picture: red at the top, green over the shields and the cannon, white elsewhere. The arcade's monitor stood on its side; its 224 × 256 picture stands upright in the middle of the LCD's 320 × 240 (2 × 2 pixels on a VGA monitor), with its always-empty first and last 8 lines left out. The buzzer plays the four-note march, the shot, the hits and the saucer's siren, and the shield's status LED is lit while the saucer is out. The 7-segment digits show the score and the credits, LEDR0..6 one LED per spare ship. SW1..SW0 (ships per game, 3 to 6) and SW2 (the extra ship at 1,000 points instead of 1,500) are the arcade's DIP switches, read when a game starts. Letters typed over the USB cable (de10.py send) play the game too, pause it, make the cannon invincible, mute it, or call up one of ten test presets: known moments of a game that the simulation draws as well (the guide lists them).

Block diagram

One clock runs the game. The game pad needs 50 MHz for the library's ADC reader; its debounced buttons are slow levels, so two flip-flops bring each one into the 25 MHz pixel clock, as they do for KEY and SW. The LCD's 100 MHz writer crosses inside the library's Screens_dispaly. There is no frame buffer: inv_draw reads the registers and the two RAMs of inv_logic directly, pixel by pixel. That is safe because they change only during a step, and a step ends long before the first visible line (at most 1,351 of the blank's 28,000 clocks). The one exception is a preset: its steps run back to back for well under a second, so the picture may tear until it pauses. The blocks are split by when they work: invaders_top at every clock, inv_logic once a frame, inv_draw at every pixel. Only the per-pixel part has to be parallel; the game itself is one sequential machine that visits each object in turn, which keeps the whole design at a seventh of the chip.

How it flows

  1. The tick and the controls. At the falling edge of VGA_VS the screen has drawn its last visible line; 35 blank lines (28,000 clocks, 1.12 ms) follow before the first visible pixel. invaders_top turns that edge into the one-clock tick. The joystick moves the cannon, A or B fires, Select (or KEY1) puts in a coin, Start (or KEY0) starts. A letter typed over USB is held for a few frames, so a step sees it as a press: l and r for 16 frames (each cancels the other), a (fire) for 3, c (coin) and s (start) for 2.
  2. One step. At the tick, inv_logic's sequencer ms walks once through its states, most of them one clock each. First the mode's work (the attract mode, the credit screen, the intro, play, game over); then, in play: the rack's edge test (is anything lit in column 213 when it moves right, or column 9 when it moves left, on lines 40..223? then the rack turns and steps down 8 lines at the end of this sweep); the cannon, 1 pixel; the shot, 4 lines up, then a probe of the 4 rows it crossed, a hit decided in the order invader, saucer, bomb, shield; one bomb slot in turn (rolling, plunger, squiggly; the squiggly's turn goes to the saucer while it flies or is due); the saucer; the next live invader moves (the arcade's ripple, one invader a step) and the march plays its next note when its timer runs out; the checks (an explosion's timer, the wave's end, the extra ship); the 27 characters of the score line and the ships line. Every what is lit here? sets p_x, p_y on the second inv_layers, the same logic that draws the screen. A burst on a shield clears its bits in the shield RAM smem; text goes into the text RAM tram. The bound is 1,351 clocks; the longest the full simulation saw was 1,064.
  3. Every pixel. As the visible lines scan, inv_draw halves the 640 × 480 counters to the LCD's 320 × 240 (lx, ly) and turns them into the arcade's pixel: x = lx − 48, y = ly + 8; the 48 columns on each side stay black. The two RAMs answer one clock late, so they are asked with the next pixel's address. inv_layers says, for that one pixel, whether the rack, an invader's explosion, the cannon, the shot, a bomb, a burst, the saucer, a shield or the ground line is lit there; the text comes from the text RAM, through font8x8 for the letters and the sprite ROMs for the icons (the cannon, the invaders, the saucer). The band the pixel is in colours it red, green or white. rgb leaves in the same clock, with no register in the way, and Screens_dispaly sends it to the VGA pins and writes it to the LCD at 100 MHz.
  4. Sound and lights. inv_logic sends one-clock triggers (a shot, a hit, the cannon's explosion, the saucer hit, the extra ship, the next march note) and holds snd_ufo high while the saucer flies. Once a frame inv_sound takes the highest-priority sound still playing and sets the half-period of one square wave: the march's G3, F3, E3, D3 (196, 175, 165, 147 Hz), the shot falling from 4.2 to 1.2 kHz, the saucer's siren between 750 Hz and 1.4 kHz; the explosions are noise from a 16-bit LFSR. The buzzer is silent when muted, during a preset and during a pause. The shield's status LED is lit while the saucer is on the screen; the 7-segment digits show the credits (HEX5, HEX4) and the score (HEX3..HEX0); LEDR0..6 light one LED per spare ship.
  5. Letters and presets. A letter typed on the PC comes through jtag_uart and debug_link: ? and F are answered there (the status line, the screen capture), and every letter also reaches invaders_top. A digit resets the game and runs a known number of steps back to back, 120 to 30,000, with LEDR7 lit meanwhile: preset 0 stays in the attract mode, 1 to 9 start a game that the invincible demo player plays, with 3 ships and the extra ship at 1,500. Then it pauses (LEDR8). p plays on from there, by hand: the demo player and the invincibility are gone. m mutes the buzzer; i makes the cannon invincible (LEDR9).

How it was tested

Without the board

sim/tb_invaders.sv runs the game, the screen and the sound together (inv_logic, inv_draw, inv_sound; everything but the board's I/O) with its own clock, tick and pixel scan, and plays one long session in 17 sections: power-up; the attract mode, its typed text and the silent demo game (the demo game lasted 2137 steps; its cannon fired 47 shots); two coins; the start and the intro; the first wave in place; the cannon's moves, its edge and the pause; the march; a shot into a shield; the first bump; columns 4 and 5 shot away through the gap between two shields (columns 4 and 5 took 547 steps); the 300-point saucer, by counting the shots (the 300-point saucer took 1225 steps (play step 2271)); the rest of the wave, shot down by a bot (45 of 45 invaders hit as the bot predicted, 990 points for the 55); the next wave, 24 lines lower; three deaths and game over, with one explosion sound per death; the hi-score and the credit screen; a second game with a low rack (below); the ten presets; the timing budget (longest step 1064 clocks; 89600 game steps). Result: PASS (89600 game steps), 89 checks, about 26 minutes in Icarus.

A monitor checks the rules after every one of the 89,600 steps: no unknown (X) bit in the game's state; the count of live invaders equals the alive bits; the cannon stays between x 16 and 185; the score never goes down; a saucer is worth the table's entry for the shots fired, shows it and adds it; the extra ship comes only at the bonus score and adds one ship; an empty wave is cleared. Rule A: every live invader is on the screen, its coordinates taken modulo 256 like the arcade's. Rule B: a lit pixel of the next live invader, from the testbench's own model of the rack, is put on a second copy of the rack's pixel logic, which must answer lit, by that invader; the same logic draws the screen and decides the shots.

Clearing a wave before it lands takes a good player. Before every step the bot plays the rack forward exactly as inv_logic will move it (one live invader a step, the bump, the drop, the freeze while an invader explodes, the shot still in flight). For the lowest invader of each column it finds where a shot fired d steps from now would meet it, through a gap in the shields and past the falling bombs, walks to the cheapest such place (edge columns first: a narrower rack drops less often) and fires when d is 0. A first, nearest-first bot let the rack land (review log).

In the second game the rack's two top rows are past line 255: the arcade's coordinates are 8 bits and wrap. The test checks that the rack is drawn (935 lit pixels, 0 of them black), shot (the shot at x 120 hit invader 38 after 98 steps), turned at the edge and landed. It came from a bug that preset 7 showed (review log). With the old rack logic put back, the test fails 1,631 checks (935 lit pixels, 755 of them black and rule B's a live invader is not drawn where it is): the test does catch that bug.

The test writes its frames the way the board's capture reads the screen (every second pixel and line, 320 × 240): attract, demo, credit, intro, play, columns, wave2, gameover, rack_low and preset_0 to preset_9, as .ppm files next to the testbench. python scripts/sim.py projects/invaders/sim/tb_invaders.sv runs it all; with -D QUICK it runs presets 0..2 only (10,093 steps, 82 checks, about 3 minutes).

On the board

The board was programmed and each preset called up over USB, the screen read back and compared with the simulation's frame: de10.py send 5, de10.py screen logs/board_5.png, screen_check.py logs/board_5.png sim/preset_5.ppm. All ten presets: 0 of 76,800 pixels differ. A first try, on an earlier build, differed in presets 1 to 9, and only in the ships row: a preset took its ships from the switches, and the board's were up. Now the switches are read when a game starts, and a preset sets its 3 ships itself.
