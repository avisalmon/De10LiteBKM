Space Invaders: architecture spec

What each module is, what crosses between them, where every bit of state lives, and the budgets (clocks per frame, memory, logic). Written for someone who will change the design or build the next game on it.

Requirements

  • The 1978 arcade game for one player: a rack of 55 invaders in 5 rows (10, 20 and 30 points) that moves one invader per frame, steps down and turns at the edges and speeds up as it thins; four shields that bombs and shots eat away pixel by pixel; one shot at a time; three kinds of bombs with the arcade's column tables and reload rates; the mystery saucer; an extra ship; later racks starting lower; the attract mode with the score table and a game played by the machine; coins, credits, game over and a high score; the coloured overlay.
  • Runs on the DE10-Lite with the LCD / game-pad shield, from the library's display chain (VGA pins and LCD together).
  • Our own pixel art at the arcade's sizes. No picture, sound or code of the original is used.
  • Playable by a person with the joystick, A, B, Select and Start (or KEY0 and KEY1), and by the AI over USB (letters), so it can be tested without hands. The screen can be read back over USB (debug_link).
  • Ten presets: a reset and a fast run to a known moment of a game. The board and the simulation must show the same picture there, pixel for pixel.
  • Every game step finishes inside the vertical blank, so a frame is always drawn from one state.
  • Fits the MAX 10 10M50 with the debug capture buffer: under 20 % of the logic, under 70 % of the memory.
  • Fully testable in simulation: a testbench plays scripted games through the whole design and renders frames.

Both RAMs answer one clock after the address. The screen therefore asks with the next scan pixel (pxl_x + 1) and uses the answer for the current one, as in Pac-Man. The sequencer sets an address, waits a clock (the _WAIT states) and then reads.

inv_layers: one answer for the screen and the game

inv_layers is pure logic: given an arcade pixel (x, y) and the objects' registers, it says which objects are lit there. It is built twice. inv_draw asks it at the beam, for every pixel of every frame; inv_logic asks it at the probe point (p_x, p_y), a few pixels per game step, to find hits and the rack's edge. Same module, same registers: what the shot hits is exactly what you see, pixel for pixel. The arcade did the same in its own way: its program tested the pixels of its frame buffer.

inv_rack_pixel: one invader moves per step

The arcade moves one invader per frame. The rack therefore has two references: invaders 0 .. cursor-1 have already moved to the new one (new_x, new_y, picture new_frame); the others are still at the old one. For a pixel, the module finds the cell under it for each reference: the column is (x - ref_x) / 16 (11 columns, 176 pixels); the row is counted up from the bottom row's last line, 16 lines per row, 8 lines of invader and 8 of gap. Both differences are taken in 8 bits and wrap, as the arcade's coordinates do: the row comes from u = ref_y + 7 - y, which runs 0..79 over the rack wherever the reference is, even past line 255. An earlier version compared the lines as plain numbers, and a rack whose bottom rows were gone vanished once it marched low (design decisions). A candidate is lit when its invader is alive, belongs to that reference (index below the cursor for the new one, at or above it for the old one) and its picture has the pixel. The kind follows the row: rows 0 and 1 the 10-point invader, rows 2 and 3 the 20-point one, row 4 the 30-point one.

During a rack's first sweep (fill) the old candidates are not drawn: a new rack appears one invader per frame, from the bottom left, like the arcade's.

inv_logic: modes, sequencer, encodings

All game state lives in one always block, run by a micro-sequencer, ms (38 states). It waits in S_IDLE. At tick (unless paused) it runs one game step: a fixed walk through the states below, most of them one clock, then back to S_IDLE. A step takes at most 1,351 clocks (every state's longest path added up; the longest of the full simulation's 89,600 steps was 1,064); the blank has 28,000 (budgets). busy is high while a step runs; the testbench checks that every step ends before the picture starts.

A probe (_COL, _WAIT, _ROW) sets p_x, p_y and the shield RAM's address, waits a clock for the RAM, then reads the layers once per clock while p_y steps through the rows. All hits are collected first and acted on in one _HIT state, so a shot that touches an invader and a shield in the same 4 rows only takes the invader.

At power-up (S_PWR, 1,024 clocks after the PLL locks) the sequencer fills the text RAM with spaces and the shield RAM with zeros, prints the header and CREDIT, and goes to S_IDLE in the attract mode.

inv_draw: the picture, one pixel at a time

The picture has no frame buffer (the 114-M9K copy inside debug_link only serves the USB capture). For every scan pixel, inv_draw works out from the game's registers whether the arcade pixel there is lit, and colours it by its overlay band. It has no registers of its own (0 in the area table): one combinational path runs from the scan counters, registered in vga_controller, to rgb, and Screens_dispaly sends it to the VGA pins and copies it to the LCD. A scan pixel lasts 40 ns. This path, ending at the capture RAM in debug_link, is the longest in the 25 MHz domain, and it still has 16.0 ns to spare (timing).

The game changes its registers and memories only during a step. A step starts at tick (the start of vertical sync) and ends long before the first visible line (budget), so every frame is drawn from one state. Only while a preset fast-forwards do steps run back to back, and then the picture may tear. The USB capture (debug_link, STEP 2) takes every second pixel of every second line: exactly the 320 × 240 art grid. The testbench writes the same rgb, from the same RTL, into .ppm files, so a board capture and a simulated frame can be compared pixel for pixel.

inv_sound: the buzzer

One square wave on the shield's buzzer (ARDUINO_IO[12]). Once per frame, at tick, the highest-priority sound that is playing sets the half-period for the coming frame, in 25 MHz clocks: half = 25,000,000 / (2 × f); 0 is silence. A counter flips the output every half clocks. For noise, the output takes a random bit at each half-period instead of flipping: a 16-bit LFSR, seed 0xACE1, feedback from bits 15, 13, 12 and 10. The arcade made its sounds with analog circuits; these are our own buzzer-sized stand-ins with the same roles and lengths. t below is the sound's timer, counting down one per frame.

The attract mode's demo game is silent too: inv_logic gates every trigger with ~attract_demo. The timers keep counting while the buzzer is muted, so unmuting picks a sound up where it would have been.

Presets. A digit makes the game reset as at power-up (full_reset: the credits and the high score are cleared too) and clear its memories. Then it runs preset_steps(n) game steps back to back, without waiting for frames (ffwd, LEDR7); meanwhile the controls count as released and the buzzer is quiet. For n ≥ 1 the game goes straight to PLAY with the demo player, invincible, 3 ships and the bonus at 1,500, whatever the switches say. At the end it pauses (LEDR8). After a game preset it also drops the invincibility and the demo player, so p carries on the same game by hand; after preset 0, p carries on the attract mode.

Budgets

Time: a game step inside the blank

A frame is 800 × 525 scan pixels at 25 MHz: 420,000 clocks, 59.52 frames per second (the arcade ran at 59.54, by MAME's numbers). A step starts at tick, the start of vertical sync. The first visible line comes 35 lines later (2 of sync, 33 of back porch): 28,000 clocks, 1.12 ms. Everything a step changes must be done by then.

A step uses less than a twentieth of the blank, and the testbench fails if one ever reaches the picture (section 17 of tb_invaders.sv). A preset is the one exception, on purpose: its steps run back to back without waiting for tick, and the picture may tear until it pauses.

The slacks are the worst of the three corners Quartus checks (slow 85 °C, slow 0 °C, fast 0 °C). Recovery and removal (the reset paths: 16.881 and 0.464 ns at worst) and minimum pulse width (4.713 ns) pass too, and the total negative slack is 0. The design's worst slack is the 25 MHz hold check, +0.065 ns at the fast corner. The 50 MHz input clock and the ADC's own 10 MHz PLL clock get only pulse-width checks. The 100 MHz setup slack is set by a path between the PLL's clocks: they come from one PLL, so the analyzer gives such a path only the time between their nearest edges. The 25 MHz scan counter reaches lcd_ctrl in 10 ns; the 100 MHz logic alone would run at 332 MHz. The buttons cross from 50 MHz into 25 MHz the same way, in 20 ns, and use 2.1 ns of it. The JTAG side of the USB link crosses to 25 MHz inside alt_jtag_atlantic.

Assumed (the sources do not say)

  • The saucer flies on line 40. A rack has landed when an invader's top reaches line 216, the cannon's line.
  • The text rows of PUSH (8), ONLY 1PLAYER BUTTON (11), PLAY PLAYER<1> (11), GAME OVER (5) and *1 PLAYER 1 COIN (17); the header (row 1), the scores (row 3), the ships and CREDIT (row 30); the score table's rows (17 to 23).
  • Bombs fall 4 lines per move, 5 when 8 or fewer invaders are left.
  • Lines 0..7 and 248..255 are always empty, so they are not shown.
  • Invaders erase the shield pixels they walk through. No plunger bomb drops while one invader is left.
  • The cannon's death: 12 pictures, 5 frames each, then 128 frames with no cannon.
  • Where a burst is drawn: a shot's at (x − 3, y − 2), a bomb's at (x − 2, y + 2).
  • Credits stop at 99. The saucer's value pointer starts at 0. The ships digit is blank at game over.
  • The machine's game starts from the table's first move every time (the sources do not say where the arcade's pointer starts).

Chosen by us

  • Every sound. The arcade made its sounds with analog circuits; ours are square waves on one buzzer (inv_sound), with the march on G3, F3, E3 and D3.
  • The waits: 64 steps between racks, 128 for PLAY PLAYER<1> and for GAME OVER.
  • The colours: red F22, green 2F2 and white FFF, in the board's 12 bits.
  • The screen: the arcade's lines 8..247 in the middle of a 320 × 240 grid, every pixel 2 × 2 on the 640 × 480 VGA picture.
  • The test aids: invincibility, pause, mute, the presets, the USB letters.

Left out

  • Two players: SCORE<2> stays 0000, and the 1 OR 2 PLAYERS messages are never shown.
  • The cocktail table (the second player's upside-down screen) and the tilt switch.
  • The analog sounds.
  • The arcade's switch that hides the coin information (bit 7 of its input port 2): ours always shows *1 PLAYER 1 COIN.
  • The first attract loop's joke, an invader that drags an upside-down Y into "PLAy": ours types PLAY straight.

Design decisions

DecisionWhyWhat it costs, or the other way
Race the beam: every pixel is worked out from the game state as the screen scans itThe arcade drew into a 1-bit 224 × 256 frame buffer and had to erase each object before drawing it again. Without a picture memory there is nothing to erase and nothing to keep in step: the screen always shows the current state.inv_draw's 1,197 LEs, and the longest 25 MHz path (16.0 ns to spare). The frame buffer would have been 57,344 bits (7 M9K) plus the drawing and erasing logic. The shields still need a memory: bursts bite them pixel by pixel.
The layers twice: inv_layers in inv_draw, and again as the probe in inv_logic A shot hits exactly what the screen shows: the game asks the same logic the picture comes from.461 LEs for the second copy. Hit boxes worked out apart from the picture could disagree with it by a pixel.
One game step per frame, in the blank, with collisions found by probing pixelsThe state never changes while a frame is drawn, so the picture never tears. A few pixels probed per step replace comparing every pair of objects.A sequencer with a state for each part of the step (the step table); at most 1,351 of the blank's 28,000 clocks.
Two rack references and a cursorThe arcade's ripple, one invader moved per frame, without storing 55 positions: invaders before the cursor are drawn at the new reference, the rest at the old one.A comparison with the cursor per pixel in inv_rack_pixel.
The arcade's 8-bit coordinates, wrappingThe arcade keeps every position in a byte. A rack whose left columns are gone has its reference below 0; one whose bottom rows are gone and that marches low has it past 255. Doing the same arithmetic keeps the rack, its edge test and its landing where the arcade's would be.Every comparison with a reference is taken modulo 256. One that was not made a low rack vanish; preset 7 showed it, and section 15 of the testbench now plays that case (with the old comparison, 1,631 of its checks fail).
One bit per pixel, the colour from bandsAs the arcade's cellophane overlay: a few comparisons on the line and the column, no colour memory.Nothing.
Lines 8..247 at 2 × 2The arcade's 256 lines do not fit the 240 of a 320 × 240 grid, and its first and last 8 are always empty.Doubling every pixel leaves 48 columns of black on each side of the 224.
Presets that always reach the same frameA digit over USB resets the game and runs a fixed number of steps with the demo player, so the board and the simulation show the same picture, and a board check is a pixel-by-pixel comparison with no hands.The steps run back to back, so the picture may tear while a preset runs.
No emulation, no original codeThe arcade's program, pictures and sounds are someone else's work. The rules were written again from the documentation, and the design is ours.Some details are assumed (see above), and the sounds are new.
The switches read when a game startsA switch flipped during a game changes nothing under the player, and the presets ignore them, so their pictures never depend on how the board was left.Flip, then start a new game.
USB letters held as levelsThe game looks at its inputs once per step, so a letter is held for 2 to 16 frames, like a press.l and r move the cannon 16 pixels; finer moves need the joystick.
