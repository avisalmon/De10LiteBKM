16 · The TinyStories LLM challenge

A language model with 15 million parameters runs on the DE10-Lite and tells children's stories on the shield's screen. It is the real thing: Karpathy's stories15M, a small Llama-2-style transformer trained on the TinyStories dataset, computed token by token on the board; with the temperature at zero, every story comes out word for word as the float32 reference in Python. The current record is 26.16 tokens per second over the official set of six stories (192 tokens in 7.34 s), measured on the board on 2026-10-31. This page is a challenge, not a recipe: it gives you the model, the Python reference that defines the right answer, the rules and the scoreboard, and nothing about how the record was built. Beat it on a DE10-Lite, any way you like.

Everything on this page is public and free: the model and tokenizer files come from the open llama2.c project (MIT), the dataset is TinyStories (Eldan and Li, 2023), and the reference script here needs only Python and numpy. The hardware design that holds the record is deliberately not published, so that everyone starts from the same place: the model, a board, and the reference.

What you see

The board is a Terasic DE10-Lite (a MAX 10 FPGA with 50k logic elements, 64 MB of SDRAM and nothing else that computes) with its LCD and game-pad shield. The pad picks a prompt and a length, Start tells the story: the words appear on the screen as the model produces them, and the bottom line counts the tokens and the speed. There is no computer in the loop while the story is told; the PC is only used before the run, to put the model's parameters into the board's memory and to read the results back for checking.

Once upon a time, there was a little girl named Lily. She loved to play outside in the sunshine. One day, she saw a big, red ball

That is the official story number 2: the prompt in bold, then exactly 32 new tokens (a token is a word or a piece of one; 32 tokens are about 25 words). The float32 reference in Python produces these words, and so must your board.

The model, every number

stories15M is one of the "tinyllamas" Andrej Karpathy trained for llama2.c on the TinyStories dataset: 2.1 million short stories written in the vocabulary of a four-year-old, made to let very small models learn to tell coherent stories. The architecture is Llama 2's, shrunk: the same blocks (RMSNorm, rotary position embeddings, multi-head attention with a key-value cache, a SwiGLU feed-forward network, a shared classifier), six layers deep, 288 wide.

A layer's matrices come to 4 × 288 × 288 + 3 × 768 × 288 = 995,328 multiply-adds per token; six layers and the 32,000 × 288 classifier give the 15,187,968 above. The classifier is 61 % of all the work and 61 % of all the weights: one token's score for every word the model knows.

The tokenizer

tokenizer.bin is Llama 2's SentencePiece vocabulary in llama2.c's flat form: an int32 with the longest piece (27 bytes), then 32,000 entries of float32 score, int32 length, bytes. Pieces starting with ▁ (U+2581) carry a leading space. Encoding is byte-pair merging: the text is split into bytes, then the pair with the best score is merged, again and again, until no pair is a known piece. A story starts with BOS (id 1); the model may end one with EOS (id 2). "Once upon a" encodes to [1, 9038, 2501, 263]; the reference script's tell --ids shows the ids of any text.

How the model works: one token's journey

A language model does one thing: given the tokens so far, it scores every token of the vocabulary as the next one. Telling a story is doing that again and again, feeding each chosen token back in. Here is what happens to one token in stories15M; the Python reference below does exactly this, in about 150 lines.

The steps, with their sizes

  1. Embedding. The token id selects one row of the 32,000 × 288 table: the token becomes a vector x of 288 numbers. No arithmetic, one lookup.
  2. RMSNorm. Before each block the vector is normalized: x · 1/√(mean(x2) + 10-5), then multiplied element by element by a learned 288-vector. It keeps the numbers in a steady range.
  3. Queries, keys, values. Three 288 × 288 matrices turn the normalized vector into q, k, v: 3 × 82,944 multiply-adds. Each is seen as 6 heads of 48 numbers.
  4. RoPE (rotary position embedding) tells attention where this token is: in each head the 48 numbers of q and of k are taken as 24 pairs, and pair i is rotated by the angle pos · 10000-2i/48. The two 256 × 24 tables in the file are the cos and sin of those angles.
  5. The KV cache. The rotated k and the v of this position are stored (per layer: 2 × 288 numbers per position, 256 positions) so that attention can look back without recomputing the past.
  6. Attention. Per head: the score of each earlier position t is q · kt / √48; softmax turns the scores into weights that sum to 1; the output is the weighted sum of the vt. That is 2 × 48 × (pos + 1) multiply-adds per head: the only work that grows with the length of the story.
  7. Output projection and residual. The six heads' outputs (288 together) go through Wo (288 × 288) and are added to the vector that entered the block. The residual stream is why six layers can be stacked: each one adds a correction instead of replacing everything.
  8. The feed-forward network (SwiGLU). RMSNorm again, then h1 = W1·x and h3 = W3·x (768 each), h = SiLU(h1) · h3 element by element with SiLU(z) = z / (1 + e-z), then W2·h back to 288, added to the stream. 3 × 221,184 = 663,552 multiply-adds: two thirds of a layer.
  9. Six times. Steps 2 to 8 are one layer; the vector goes through six of them with their own weights.
  10. The classifier. A last RMSNorm, then the vector is dotted with every row of the embedding table: 32,000 scores (logits), 9,216,000 multiply-adds, 61 % of the token's work.
  11. Choosing. At temperature 0 the next token is simply the highest score (on a tie, the lowest id). With a temperature above 0 the scores would be divided by it and sampled from; the challenge uses 0 so that there is exactly one right story.
  12. Prompt and generation. The prompt's tokens (BOS first) are pushed through one by one just to fill the caches; from the last prompt token on, every output is appended and fed back. A story ends at EOS or at the length asked for; the caches hold 256 positions, so a story is never longer than that.

The reference: what "the same story" means

ref/tinystories_ref.py is the whole definition of a right answer. It is llama2.c's run.c written in plain numpy, float32, in the same order of operations, with the temperature fixed at 0. It needs Python 3.8 or newer and numpy, nothing else. It downloads the model, tells stories, prints every fact used on this page, writes the official set and checks a challenger's stories against it.

pip install numpy
cd examples/16_llm_challenge/ref
python tinystories_ref.py download            # stories15M.bin (58 MB) + tokenizer.bin, hashed
python tinystories_ref.py selftest            # tokenizer and model against known answers: PASS
python tinystories_ref.py info                # the header, every tensor, the counts, the hashes
python tinystories_ref.py tell "Once upon a"  # a story of 32 new tokens (--new 100 for more, --ids)
python tinystories_ref.py official --check    # reference_stories.json equals a fresh float32 run

tell streams the words as they are chosen and ends with the speed, for example 32 new tokens in 0.3 s on this PC (126.0 tok/s, numpy float32): that is the number your board competes with the organizer on, not with the PC. tell --temperature 0.8 shows what sampling does to the same prompt; the challenge does not use it.

Why 32 tokens and not whole stories: 32 tokens per prompt is enough to make guessing impossible (six specific sequences of 32 choices out of 32,000) and short enough that an entry can be verified in seconds on any design, including one that reads the results out over USB by hand. The organizer's record was measured on exactly this set.

Give the new tokens as ids (new_tokens) or as text (generated_text: the story minus the prompt, so it starts with the space before the first new word; a missing leading space is forgiven); ids are safer. seconds is the time of that story as defined in the rules. The score is the 192 tokens divided by the sum of the six times. Send the file, your video and a short description of what you built to the address at the end of the page.

The rules

  1. The board is a DE10-Lite. A Terasic DE10-Lite as sold (MAX 10 10M50DAF484C7G, 64 MB SDRAM, 50 MHz oscillator). The model is stored and computed on it: its FPGA, its SDRAM, its flash. The LCD / game-pad shield is welcome but not required; show the story on the LCD, on a VGA monitor, or send it as text over the USB-Blaster cable. The PC may put the model into the board before the run and read the results after it; while a story is told, nothing outside the board computes.
  2. It is this model. stories15M as published (the hashes above), all 15,191,712 parameters, the same tokenizer. How you build it, how you store the model and how you compute is entirely up to you: the only judge is the output.
  3. Temperature 0. Greedy decoding: the most probable token every time, the lowest id on a tie, exactly as tinystories_ref.py does. There is one right story per prompt.
  4. The stories are the reference's. The six official prompts, 32 new tokens each, must come out identical to reference_stories.json, token for token. check is the judge.
  5. Real, and measured. A story's time runs from the moment the prompt is complete and the board is told to start to the moment the last of its 32 new tokens is out; the prompt's own processing is inside it. Loading the model before the run is not. Each of the six stories runs from a clean start (no state kept from an earlier story), and every token is computed by the board from the model during the run: no stored answers. Your design must take any prompt, not only the six; when the organizer verifies an entry he also tries prompts of his own, which are not scored but must be stories.
  6. The score is 192 tokens divided by the sum of the six times, in tokens per second, two decimals. Measure with the board's own clock or with the PC's, and say which; the organizer re-measures.
  7. An entry is the JSON above, a video of the board telling at least one official story with the time visible, and a few lines about what you built, as many as you care to reveal. The scoreboard lists an entry after the organizer has seen it run: on your board at a meeting, on a board he lends you, or from a bitstream and loading procedure you send.
  8. No hints. The organizer's design is not published while the challenge runs and no implementation advice is given: everyone starts from this page. Questions about the rules are welcome by mail and the answers are added here for all.

Why the rules are shaped like this: a language model is a deterministic machine once the temperature is 0, so "the same story" is a sharp test that anyone can run in a minute, while "nothing outside the board computes" and "any prompt" keep it a real machine and not a recording. The time includes the prompt because a design that is slow to start should not hide it.
